# **Comparative Impact of Thermal-Zoning Resolution in Urban Building Energy Modelling: A Critical Assessment of EUI Accuracy, Sizing Predictands, and Scale Trade-offs**

## **Quantitative EUI and Thermal Load Sensitivity of Spatial Grouping**

The spatial subdivision of buildings into discrete mathematical volumes, or thermal zones, is a foundational step in the preparation of physics-based building energy models1. Modellers frequently aggregate spaces to ease computational demand and simplify geometry, yet this abstraction inevitably alters simulated thermodynamic behaviours3. A systematic evaluation of published sensitivity literature reveals that the errors introduced by thermal-zoning simplifications vary dramatically between building typologies, climatic boundary conditions, and the specific energy end-uses being predicted3.  
In residential sectors, simplifying a multi-room layout to a single zone per building or a single zone per floor consistently leads to a systematic underestimation of annual space-heating demand3. This underestimation is primarily driven by the mathematical assumption of instantaneous, uniform air mixing across the simplified zone volume, which artificially dissipates localized thermal barriers and masks spatial temperature variations6. In semi-detached residential dwellings in cold climates, reducing zones to a single zone per building underestimates annual heating energy by up to 26.0%, whereas a one-zone-per-floor scheme yields an underestimation of approximately 17.0%3. For multi-family residential structures, aggregating room-level models into floor-level models introduces a mean absolute error in annual heating demand of 10.6% to 14.0%3. When zoning strategies merge spaces across different floors, the resulting changes in Gross Floor Area calculations and temperature uniformity further compress predicted heating energy loads8. Even in warmer climates, simplifying detailed space-by-space residential models to a single-zone model yields deviations in total annual energy demand and predicted thermal comfort of up to 20.0%5.  
In commercial offices, where core and perimeter zones experience highly divergent solar and internal load profiles, standardizing the floorplate into a core-and-perimeter configuration is a common practice prescribed by ASHRAE 90.1 Appendix-G9. However, when this standardized perimeter-core configuration is validated against actual room-by-room architectural floor plans, severe discrepancies emerge11. Across a wide range of distinct morphological floor plans, the core-and-perimeter zoning scheme produces a deceptively low Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 15.0% for total annual Energy Use Intensity (EUI), alongside a Mean Bias Error (MBE) of 2.0%11. This low annual EUI error masks extreme, compensating discrepancies among individual end-use load components11. Specifically, the core-and-perimeter model exhibits a highly significant RMSE of 175.0% for annual heating loads and 105.0% for annual cooling loads11. These errors are further amplified in specific floor layouts. For instance, in layout configurations where the proportions of core and perimeter regions differ from the standardized 5-metre depth rule, heating and cooling predictions deviate by an average of 124.0% and 265.0% respectively11. Additionally, floor plans with edge circulation acting as an unconditioned buffer zone show massive energy deviations, as core-and-perimeter models fail to capture this thermal buffering effect11.  
At the urban scale, the impact of thermal-zoning resolution is heavily dampened by spatial aggregation12. For example, the Stockholm-based MUBES tool demonstrated that at the single-building scale, changing zoning resolution from single-zone to core-perimeter variants alters Thermal Energy Demand Intensity (TEDI) by approximately 10.0%14. However, at the aggregated district scale, the total TEDI variation across two distinct neighborhoods drops to less than 2.3%12.

## **Relative Ranking of Annual EUI Accuracy Drivers**

In the broader context of building energy sensitivity, spatial thermal-zoning resolution is classified as a **second-order driver** of annual whole-building EUI20. While zoning significantly shapes peak thermodynamics, annual energy totals are dominated by operational schedules, HVAC equipment efficiencies, internal heat gains, and envelope thermal performance23.  
The table below presents a comparative hierarchy of annual EUI accuracy drivers compiled from variance-based global sensitivity analyses.

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **1** | **HVAC System Configuration and Controls** | System type (VAV vs. FCU vs. VRF), efficiency metrics (COP/part-load curves), minimum flow ratios, and sequence of operations. | **30.0% to \>50.0%** | \[cite: 21, 25, 26, 27\] |
| **2** | **Occupant Behaviour and Operational Schedules** | Stochastic presence, dynamic appliance and lighting use schedules, thermostat setpoints, and window-opening behaviours. | **10.0% to 35.0%** | \[cite: 28, 29, 30, 31\] |

| **4** | **Thermophysical Envelope Characteristics** | Window-to-wall ratio (WWR), glazing solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC), wall/roof R-values, and external shading. | **10.0% to 25.0%** | \[cite: 21, 33, 34, 35\] |
| **5** | **Spatial Thermal Zoning Resolution** | Spatial partition granularity (room-by-room vs. floor-by-floor vs. core-perimeter vs. single-zone). | **5.0% to 15.0%** *(Building scale)* **\< 2.3%** *(District scale)* | \[cite: 4, 11, 12, 17\] |

This hierarchy indicates that the mechanical system configuration represents the most critical parameter in whole-building energy prediction25. The selection of HVAC typologies (for example, switching from a direct expansion VAV system to a chiller-based VAV or four-pipe FCU system) can alter annual energy consumption and EUI by up to 41.5% under identical weather and internal load profiles25. Similarly, control sequence retrofits, such as implementing optimized airside sequences, yield EUI variations averaging 31.0%27.  
Occupant behaviour and associated operational schedules constitute the second most critical driver29. Deterministic standards often ignore the stochastic variability of real-world occupants, which can introduce uncalibrated single-building EUI errors of up to 30.0%28. Transitioning from static profiles to stochastic schedules derived from measured occupancy shifts annual energy predictions by more than 12.0%31, with adaptive window and lighting interactions further modifying heating demand by approximately 4.0% to 7.0%29.  
Internal gains, specifically EPD and LPD, are consistently highlighted in global sensitivity analyses as highly sensitive variables20. Because modern, code-compliant envelopes are highly insulated, building cooling demands are increasingly driven by internal loads32. Underestimating lighting or plug loads represents a major source of EUI prediction bias28, while incorporating diversity factors to account for non-coincident internal loads alters peak cooling predictions by 7.0%32.  
Thermophysical envelope characteristics, such as the solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC), window-to-wall ratio (WWR), insulation thickness, and orientation, demonstrate high to moderate importance21. Envelope optimization across severe climates can reduce baseline operational EUI by 15.0% to 25.0%34.  
In contrast, spatial thermal-zoning resolution has a minor impact on annual energy outputs12. Because EUI is integrated over 8,760 hours, spatial temperature errors and load overpredictions often cancel out over seasonal cycles11. Thus, zoning resolution is classified as a second-order driver of annual EUI, whereas it remains a first-order driver for peak sizing and local thermal comfort4.

## **Interplay of Geometry, Predictand Metrics, and Sizing Requirements**

The sensitivity of a building energy model to thermal-zoning resolution is heavily dependent on the interaction between building geometry, local climate, and the targeted output metrics.

### **Geometric Plan Depth and Climate Context**

The physical mechanism that renders simplified zoning inaccurate is load cancellation4. In deep-plan buildings, a substantial core area exists that has no direct contact with the exterior envelope10. This core zone is dominated entirely by internal heat gains from equipment, lighting, and occupants, making it a cooling-dominated environment year-round32. In contrast, the surrounding perimeter zones are highly sensitive to solar radiation, wind, and outdoor temperature fluctuations4.  
When such a building is modeled using a simplified single-zone or one-zone-per-floor scheme, these thermodynamic boundaries are eliminated4. Within a single thermal zone, the simulation engine calculates a uniform air temperature based on a single air node6. This mathematical assumption allows convective heat gains generated in the core to instantly offset convective heat losses occurring at the envelope perimeter6.  
In cold climates, such as Chicago, this load cancellation artificially reduces both heating and cooling load calculations, leading to a substantial underprediction of annual energy demands4. Specifically, in cold climates, using a simplified floor-by-floor zoning method results in underpredicting natural gas heating use by 3.7% to 4.9% under standard layouts, and up to 14.2% when using prototype shapes4. Conversely, in tropical climates, such as Singapore, the ambient temperature remains consistently high, meaning that all core and perimeter zones require cooling simultaneously4. Because there are no opposing heating and cooling demands to cancel out, the difference in annual cooling demand between a detailed core-perimeter model and a simplified floor-by-floor model is minimal, typically remaining around 3.0%4. For high-rise structures, simulating each floor explicitly is redundant if the building has a uniform vertical program36. Utilizing a floor-multiplier technique (simulating only the bottom, top, and a representative middle floor and scaling the results) introduces an annual source energy error of only 2.6% while cutting simulation runtimes by a factor of two to three4.

### **Predictand Discrepancies: Annual EUI versus Peak Sizing and Comfort**

The required level of zoning resolution is highly dependent on whether the model is intended to predict annual EUI, peak equipment sizing, or thermal comfort:

* **Annual EUI:** Simplified zoning strategies provide sufficient accuracy5. Because annual EUI is an integrated sum over seasonal cycles, local temperature variations and transient load overpredictions tend to average out, keeping EUI errors within a 5.0% to 15.0% margin11.  
* **Peak Sizing and Equipment Capacity:** Detailed zoning is critical1. HVAC systems are sized based on extreme, non-coincident peak demands occurring in localized zones32. Aggregated zoning models smooth out these localized peaks, leading to significant system under-sizing4. For example, a simplified floor-by-floor model underpredicts peak fan capacity by 15.2%, peak cooling capacity by 11.1%, and peak heating capacity by 11.0% compared to a core-perimeter baseline4. In office buildings with complex layouts, standard core-and-perimeter schemes underpredict peak heating and cooling capacities by up to 175.0% and 105.0% respectively compared to detailed room-by-room models11. Peak autosized capacity variations under floor-multiplier simplifications span wide ranges, with cooling capacity deviations of \-5.5% to \+10.2% and fan capacity deviations of \-4.9% to \+12.2%4.  
* **Thermal Comfort:** Detailed zoning is mandatory7. Comfort metrics, such as PMV and PPD, are highly sensitive to local air velocities, mean radiant temperatures, and relative humidity41. Simplified models assume spatial uniformity, preventing the simulation engine from identifying localized overheating, cooling drafts, or unmet load hours6.

## **Computational Cost-Benefit and Uncertainty Propagation at Scale**

When deploying Urban Building Energy Modelling (UBEM) at a neighborhood or city scale, the selection of thermal-zoning resolution requires balancing computational execution time against the propagation of input uncertainties12.

### **Computational Overheads of Zoning Resolution**

The transition from a simplified one-zone-per-floor model to a 5-zone core-perimeter scheme multiplies the number of thermal zones and surfaces in the simulation12. Physics-based engines (such as EnergyPlus) calculate convective and radiative heat transfers by solving high-dimensional system matrices for every zone air node and surface boundary12. Increasing zoning resolution causes simulation times to grow exponentially12.  
Automated geometry algorithms can construct core-perimeter boundaries quickly46. Tools such as *AutoZoner* and the *Convex-Partitioning-Zoner (CPZ)* can automatically split concave footprints into convex core-perimeter zones in less than 500 milliseconds per floorplate46. Similarly, the *Multizone Assignment Algorithm (MZA)* utilizes binary space partitioning to automate zoning when spatial layouts are unknown47. However, while the geometric construction runtime is minimal, the subsequent simulation runtimes for these detailed models remain a major bottleneck when scaling to tens of thousands of buildings12.  
The *Shoeboxer* algorithm addresses this bottleneck by clustering building facades based on solar microclimates and mapping the results to representative core-perimeter shoebox models17. This technique maintains annual EUI errors within a 5.0% to 10.0% margin while reducing simulation execution times by a factor of 29617. At the district scale, since the aggregated thermal energy demand variation between detailed and simplified models is less than 2.3%, utilizing floor-by-floor zoning with floor multipliers is the most computationally efficient choice for UBEM workflows12.

### **Dominated by Operational Uncertainty**

Archetype-based UBEMs typically assign building properties using deterministic templates due to a lack of building-specific information28. The uncalibrated parameters in these templates—specifically HVAC system efficiencies, operating hours, thermostat setpoints, and plug-load schedules—are first-order drivers of EUI variance, individually responsible for EUI variations of 30.0% to over 50.0%25.  
In the presence of these massive, uncalibrated operational uncertainties, investing computational budget into detailed geometric zoning yields minimal benefit39. Achieving high geometric accuracy (such as moving to room-level subdivisions) does not improve model accuracy if the operational schedules or HVAC system types are incorrect39. In fact, detailed models can generate misleading results by masking underlying operational errors through compensating geometric errors29. Therefore, for uncalibrated UBEM applications, utilizing a simplified floor-by-floor or single-zone-per-floor zoning strategy is the most reasonable approach10.

## **Analytical Verdict**

A thorough evaluation of empirical simulation literature establishes that thermal-zoning resolution is a second-order driver of annual whole-building EUI accuracy, with annual deviations typically constrained between 5.0% and 15.0%4. Consequently, upgrading from simplified floor-by-floor schemas to a detailed core-and-perimeter configuration is mathematically incapable of closing a 45.0% single-building performance gap, as such massive discrepancies are dominated by first-order input uncertainties11. To resolve an error of this scale, the UBEM calibration process must prioritise primary EUI drivers, specifically validating HVAC equipment configurations, actual operating schedules, and internal load intensities, which individually sway EUI by 30.0% to over 50.0%25.

## **Analytical Confidence and Identified Research Gaps**

### **Areas of High Confidence**

* **District Aggregation Dampening Effect:** There is high confidence that spatial aggregation across a district dampens zoning-related errors, resulting in EUI deviations of less than 2.3% at the district scale12.  
* **Peak Load Underestimation:** There is high confidence that simplified zoning strategies consistently underpredict peak HVAC sizing loads and equipment capacities by 10.0% to 15.0%4.  
* **Efficacy of Floor Multipliers:** There is high confidence that utilizing floor multipliers for multi-storey buildings provides high EUI accuracy (within 2.6% error) while reducing computational demands by a factor of two to three4.

### **Critical Gaps and Epistemic Uncertainties**

* **Dynamic Interzonal Airflow Modelling:** Standard multi-zone models assume adiabatic internal boundaries or simple conduction across partition walls, completely neglecting convective air mixing through open doors and hallways6. This convective heat transfer can be up to 25 times more effective than conduction6. The lack of reliable macroscopic airflow network data in UBEMs represents a major gap in representing localized thermal demands accurately6.  
* **Stochastic, Multi-Zone Occupant Behaviours:** Current UBEMs apply uniform occupancy schedules across aggregated zones, ignoring how occupant movement between rooms, localized window openings, and adaptive behaviours dynamically alter convective boundary conditions and thermal load distributions29.  
* **Microclimatic Integration at Scale:** There is a lack of integration between dynamic internal zoning and localized microclimatic variations, such as street-canyon aerodynamics, urban heat island intensity, and transient long-wave radiation exchanges between neighboring facades45. These microclimatic factors can alter localized heating and cooling demands by up to 10.0%, yet they are rarely coupled with zoning configurations in automated UBEM workflows12.
