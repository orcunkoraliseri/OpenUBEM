# **Thermally Ideal but Mechanically Deficient: Resolving the Service End-Use Gap in Urban Building Energy Modelling**

## **Mathematical Mechanics and Omissions of the Ideal-Loads Air System**

The transition from single-building performance simulation to Urban Building Energy Modelling (UBEM) requires simplified thermodynamic representations to preserve computational resources when simulating thousands of structures1. In EnergyPlus-based workflows, this simplification is achieved through the implementation of the ZoneHVAC:IdealLoadsAirSystem component3. While this component is highly effective at calculating raw space conditioning requirements, it introduces a systemic discrepancy when compared to detailed, physics-based HVAC models due to its fundamental mathematical formulation.  
According to the *EnergyPlus Engineering Reference*, the ideal-loads system is "modeled as an ideal VAV terminal unit with variable supply temperature and humidity"5. The underlying algorithm calculates the air mass flow rate required to meet zone sensible loads, adjusting the mixed-air conditions by combining outdoor air and zone exhaust or recirculation air5. The core mechanism is defined by the *Input/Output Reference*, which states:  
"This component can be thought of as an ideal unit that mixes air at the zone exhaust condition with the specified amount of outdoor air and then adds or removes heat and moisture at 100% efficiency in order to produce a supply air stream at the specified conditions. The energy required to condition the supply air is metered and reported as DistrictHeating and DistrictCooling."3  
Because the energy transfer occurs at a theoretical 100% thermal efficiency, the system operates entirely outside the boundaries of real-world mechanical configurations3. It does not model fluid loops, central plant equipment, or mechanical movement3. Consequently, all energy required to condition the supply air stream is metered and reported strictly under virtual utility meters: DistrictHeating and DistrictCooling3. This formulation inherently omits the mechanical and auxiliary components necessary to distribute air and water, generate domestic hot water, or support auxiliary HVAC operations8.  
The table below outlines which energy end-uses are dynamically represented by the ZoneHVAC:IdealLoadsAirSystem and which are omitted.

| End-Use Category | Modeled by IdealLoadsAirSystem? | Primary Representation Mechanism / Note |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Space Heating (Sensible)** | Yes | Calculated as the sensible heating required to meet the thermostat setpoint; reported as DistrictHeating3. |
| **Space Cooling (Sensible)** | Yes | Calculated as the sensible cooling required to meet the thermostat setpoint; reported as DistrictCooling3. |
| **Humidification (Latent Heating)** | Yes | Calculated when a humidistat is present and humidification control is active; metered as DistrictHeating3. |
| **Dehumidification (Latent Cooling)** | Yes | Controlled via humidistat or constant sensible heat ratio (SHR); metered as DistrictCooling3. |
| **Outdoor Air / Economiser** | Yes | Calculates the sensible and latent impact of ventilation air; models economiser damper logic and heat recovery cycles3. |
| **Supply / Return Fans** | **No** | Kinetic energy of air distribution, fan motor heat gain, and fan pressure drops are entirely omitted8. |
| **Hydronic Pumps** | **No** | Water loop circulation, loop friction losses, and pump motor electrical consumption are bypassed3. |
| **Service Hot Water (DHW)** | **No** | Bypassed; requires separate water-use equipment and plant loop definitions10. |
| **Commercial Refrigeration** | **No** | Bypassed; requires explicit refrigeration-specific component models11. |
| **HVAC Auxiliaries / Parasitics** | **No** | Crankcase heaters, cooling tower basin heaters, and control board parasitics are not simulated8. |

## **Structural Discrepancies in the Annual Building Utility Performance Summary Output**

When executing a simulation in EnergyPlus, the Annual Building Utility Performance Summary (ABUPS) "End Uses" table serves as the primary output for energy accounting. Contrasting this table between an ideal-loads model and a fully detailed HVAC model of the same building reveals a structural redistribution of energy across fuel types and categories.  
In a fully detailed HVAC model (such as a commercial prototype utilizing a packaged VAV system with a boiler and chiller), energy consumption is distributed across multiple columns and fuel rows6. Heating and cooling energy are metered directly under the primary fuel source—typically Electricity for chillers and DX coils, and Natural Gas or Electricity for boilers and reheat coils6. Auxiliary HVAC energy is populated across specific functional rows: Fans captures the electrical consumption of supply, return, and exhaust fans14; Pumps captures primary, secondary, and condenser water circulation loop pumps14; and Heat Rejection captures cooling tower fan and spray pump energy14.  
In an ideal-loads simulation, the ABUPS table undergoes several structural changes:

* **Utility Shifts:** The columns for Heating and Cooling under standard fuel rows such as Electricity and Natural Gas are structurally zero or near-zero15. Instead, the entire space conditioning thermal load is relocated to the District Heating and District Cooling rows3.  
* **Zeroed Mechanical Rows:** Because the ideal-loads system bypasses the physical representation of mechanical loops, the Fans, Pumps, and Heat Rejection rows are structurally zero8.  
* **Decoupled Water Systems:** The Water Systems row (representing service hot water) is zero unless separate, decoupled water-heating systems are explicitly defined10.  
* **Unaltered Baseloads:** Non-HVAC end-use rows, specifically Interior Lighting, Exterior Lighting, and Interior Equipment, remain identical between the two modeling methodologies, as they are defined as zone-level internal gains and are unaffected by the HVAC system choice10.

This structural shifting creates an immediate deficit in "Other" and "Auxiliary" end-uses, as the dynamic mechanical consumption is completely unrepresented in the raw EnergyPlus output8.

## **Encodings and Post-Processing Workflows for Recovering Service Loads in UBEM**

To bridge the service-load deficit without incurring the computational and data-collection costs of detailing HVAC systems for thousands of buildings, urban-scale modelling platforms employ several distinct workflows1.

### **Auxiliary and Empirical Post-Processing**

The most common approach scales the raw thermal heating and cooling loads generated by ZoneHVAC:IdealLoadsAirSystem using empirical coefficient of performance (COP) and efficiency curves, subsequently adding fixed energy allocations for fans, pumps, and service water heating based on historical or code-minimum benchmarks8. Platforms such as DesignBuilder (in its Simple HVAC mode) apply seasonal efficiency factors to heating and cooling, then use a scheduled Watts-per-square-metre (![][image1]) input to separately calculate pump and auxiliary equipment energy8. Similarly, workflows utilizing CBECS datasets apply building-specific, end-use percentage allocations to back-calculate the missing service energy19.

### **Decoupled Sub-System Modelling**

Instead of omitting service water heating, hybrid workflows insert standalone thermal or electrical objects directly into the zone definitions10. In the MIT Urban Modeling Interface (UMI) workflow, water heating is modeled using decoupled WaterUse:Equipment objects10. UMI simulates the thermal envelope using simplified zone "shoeboxes" to capture district heating and cooling demands, but maps separate water heating energy rates directly to the final building-level Energy Use Intensity (EUI) calculations10.

### **Room Ventilation and Dynamic Fan Energy Approximation**

To avoid the zero-fan-energy limitation while using ideal loads, some workflows decouple space conditioning from outdoor air delivery8. Mechanical ventilation is modeled using standalone ZoneVentilation:DesignFlowRate objects8. This permits the inclusion of fan kinetic energy and the thermal impact of fan heat pickup in the zone heat balance, while space heating and cooling are still managed by the ideal-loads air system8.

### **External Systems Sizing and Co-simulation**

Advanced district-scale workflows extract the hourly thermal heating and cooling demand profiles generated by the EnergyPlus ideal-loads system and pass them as inputs to an external system-sizing tool27. In platforms like URBANopt, the GeoJSON-to-Modelica Translator (GMT) is utilized to model district heating and cooling networks, geothermal borefields, and central heat pumps30. This allows the mechanical loads (pumps, central plant auxiliaries) to be dynamically simulated in Modelica using the high-resolution, physics-based thermal loads generated by EnergyPlus30.

### **Explicit Internal Gains Injection**

For smaller ancillary process loads, such as refrigeration or HVAC parasitic controllers, modellers inject equivalent heat gains using ElectricEquipment, OtherEquipment, or Exterior:Lights objects directly into the zone or exterior environment templates8. These objects operate on predefined schedules to represent the baseload energy signatures of transformers, crankcase heaters, or small localized refrigeration units, ensuring their electrical consumption is captured in the final ABUPS balance sheets8.

## **Thermodynamic Non-Recoverability: The Fallacy of Zoning and Basis Corrections**

It is a common misconception that the service-load deficit can be recovered by altering the spatial zoning resolution of a UBEM or by applying a blanket coefficient of performance (COP) or fuel "basis correction" to the heating and cooling outputs. Changing the zoning resolution—such as transitioning from single-zone layouts to core-perimeter zoning at 2-metre or 3-metre depths—only serves to refine the spatial calculation of solar heat gains, internal gains, and envelope conduction1. This spatial refinement alters the raw thermal loads of the zones1, but it does not simulate the mechanical systems required to deliver that thermal energy.  
Similarly, a COP or basis correction simply scales the thermal energy required for space conditioning to estimate the primary source energy8. This corrective scaling does not account for the auxiliary energy consumption of fans and pumps, which is governed by fluid-dynamic laws rather than thermal demands31. Under real-world conditions, fan and pump energy are functions of volumetric flow rates, system pressure drops, and motor efficiencies, which scale non-linearly with system operation30. Furthermore, service water heating and commercial refrigeration represent completely independent thermodynamic pathways10. Water heating is an open-loop mass-transfer process driven by occupant consumption patterns10, while refrigeration is a closed-loop vapor-compression process dedicated to localized thermal envelopes11. Neither of these processes scales with space-conditioning thermal loads, rendering them mathematically unreachable by any space conditioning basis correction.

## **Verdict on the Ideal-Loads Deficit**

The approximately 42% "Other" deficit observed when comparing ideal-loads UBEM simulations to fully detailed mechanical models is an inherent, expected consequence of the mathematical formulation of ZoneHVAC:IdealLoadsAirSystem3. Because the ideal-loads system operates at a theoretical 100% efficiency and completely bypasses the simulation of physical air and water distribution loops, it structurally zeros out all fan, pump, auxiliary, and water-heating energy rows in the annual utility reports3. Changing zoning resolution only refines zone-level thermal load calculations1, and COP-based basis corrections only scale space-conditioning energy8. Consequently, these unmodeled mechanical, fluid-dynamic, and process-specific energy demands remain thermodynamically unreachable without implementing decoupled sub-system models or statistical post-processing workflows8.

## **Confidence and Gaps**

The confidence in the thermodynamic characterisation of the ZoneHVAC:IdealLoadsAirSystem is extremely high, as it is based on the primary source code and documentation of EnergyPlus3. The quantitative service-load shares are highly reliable on a national weighted average basis, as they are derived from PNNL prototype models and CBECS statistical datasets18.  
However, a notable data gap exists in the dynamic, climate-dependent interaction between auxiliary loads and thermal loads. While hybrid UBEM workflows can approximate fan and pump energy using static schedules or post-simulation multipliers8, they fail to capture the real-time feedback loops present in detailed HVAC models, such as how fan motor heat dissipation directly reduces heating loads and increases cooling loads within the thermal zone8.  
Additionally, the reliance of UBEM on generalized archetypes introduces regional uncertainties; local variances in building maintenance, operating schedules, and system degradations can cause actual auxiliary energy consumption to deviate significantly from idealized, code-minimum prototype baselines16.
