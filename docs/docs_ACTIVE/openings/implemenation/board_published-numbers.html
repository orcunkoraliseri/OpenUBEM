10 August — the full run is finished, and the surprise is that it finished without anyone watching it. You asked me to carry on submitting to the cluster. I found the queue empty and assumed work had been lost. It had not: everything had already run. All 60 job groups, all 40,800 building simulations, of which 40,755 succeeded — 99.89%. Nothing timed out, nothing ran out of memory at the job level, nothing was cancelled. So I submitted nothing, and that was the right call rather than a cautious one: resubmitting would have spent days of cluster time recomputing results we already had.

The 45 failures are a property of those 45 buildings, not bad luck — and we can prove it by accident. Eight of the job groups had been submitted twice by mistake. Both times, the same buildings failed, in the same numbers. A run that repeats its own failures exactly is a run you can trust to repeat its successes. An accidental double-submission turned into a free reproducibility check, which is the kind of evidence you normally have to plan for.

The results are now on your machine, and the count matches on both sides. 40,800 building folders came back, with a complete set of files for each. Every one of the 40,800 has the floor-area file we need for the measurement question still open, in all five methods. That work is no longer waiting on data.

I checked the failures in both directions, which is the check that actually means something. Counting failures in the results and counting them in the cluster's own records both give 45, and every one of the 45 matches a specific named building. Nothing failed quietly on one side and not the other — zero unexplained in either direction. That is the difference between "we found 45 failures" and "there are 45 failures".

What I did not learn, said plainly: for 43 of the 45 we know which building failed and nothing about why. One clue is already visible: one small rural neighbourhood accounts for 24 of the 45 across three unrelated methods — roughly 4.7% there against 0.11% everywhere else. Failures clustering in one place across methods that share nothing points at the input data for those buildings. That is a hypothesis, not yet a measurement.

Where this stands, in plain language

  1. You picked the numbers that could already be wrong. That is four problems, and they do not share a fix. Two of them are about energy per square metre: we divide a building's energy by a floor area we assumed, and for most buildings the simulation was not actually run on that floor area. One is about the energy itself: every building's lights and equipment are modelled as if built to 2022 code, whatever year it was really built. The fourth is about a measuring instrument: the score that says how often the building-type guesser is right slid four points and nobody knows why. Grouping them was your call and it was the right one — they are the only items on the register that can make a published result wrong rather than merely incomplete.
  2. All the measuring is done, and the news on 6 August is good. The two big unfixed problems both live in one method of dividing a building into rooms — and that method cannot be picked by accident, only by name. I checked all 16,320 buildings behind our adopted results: not one used it. So the numbers this project stands on are not affected. The two problems are not smaller than they were; we now know where they stop, and it is short of anything we have published as a headline.
  3. Most of the decisions are now taken; two remain.
